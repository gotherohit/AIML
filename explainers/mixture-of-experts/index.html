Mixture of experts: why a 1-trillion-parameter model uses 5% per token

Mistral Large 4 has 1.05 trillion parameters but uses only 52 billion for each token. This short article explains the high-level idea behind it: a router that runs only a few experts per token.

In one line: a mixture-of-experts model stores many blocks of numbers but runs only a few of them for each token, so it can be huge without every token costing more. "1 trillion parameters" does not mean 1 trillion per token.

1 trillion parameters, 5% used

On 6 October 2026 Mistral launched a public preview of Mistral Large 4 ("Unofficially ML4, very officially: le Chonk"). Its model card lists 1.05 trillion parameters, but only 52 billion active. 52 out of 1,050 is about 5%. So what does "active" mean, and what is the other 95% doing?

The normal kind: dense models

A parameter is one number the model learns during training. In a normal model, called dense, every token passes through every layer, and each layer has two parts: attention (the token looks at other tokens) and a feed-forward block (the token is processed on its own). The token uses all of both. So in a dense model, more parameters means more work for every token: slower, costlier answers.

Mixture of experts: split the block, pick a few

A mixture of experts breaks that link. Take Mixtral, an earlier Mistral model, whose paper documents it fully. In each layer, the one feed-forward block is replaced by 8 separate blocks, called experts, with a small router in front of them:

  1. For each token, the router scores all 8 experts.
  2. It keeps the top 2, and only those 2 run.
  3. Their outputs are blended, weighted by the scores. The other 6 do nothing for that token.
  4. Next token, new scores, maybe a different pair, at every layer.

Attention is not split: it is shared and always runs. That's why Mixtral's active count is more than 2/8 of its total:

Total parameters (stored)Active parameters (used per token)
Mixtral47B13B
Mistral Large 41.05T52B

That's the trick. In the Mixtral paper's words, "one can increase the model's parameter count while keeping its computational cost effectively constant."

I tested it on a tiny Mixtral

I ran Hugging Face's small test copy of the Mixtral architecture (hf-internal-testing/Mixtral-tiny: 2 layers, 8 experts, 2 picked per token) on a laptop:

  • The router: on a 10-token sentence, every token picked exactly 2 of the 8 experts; in layer 1 it used 8 different pairs.
  • The work: 10 tokens × 2 layers × 2 experts = 40 expert runs. If all 8 ran every time, it would be 160.
  • The proof: for one token, I overwrote every expert it did not pick with random noise: 132,120,576 numbers, 53.5% of the model. The output changed by exactly 0.0. The unpicked experts really did nothing.
What this test shows. The test model's weights are not trained. It proves the mechanism (which experts run, and that the others have no effect); it says nothing about which experts a trained model would choose.

One myth: experts are not subject specialists

The word "expert" suggests a maths expert and a poetry expert. The Mixtral authors checked: "we do not observe obvious patterns in the assignment of experts based on the topic." Routing followed syntax more than subject; in code, for example, "the indentation tokens are always assigned to the same experts". Think of an expert as one of several blocks of numbers the router can choose, not as a specialist.

Scaling it up

The idea isn't new: a 2017 paper by Shazeer et al. built "a MoE with up to 137 billion parameters". Mistral's own models show the trade at scale:

ModelTotalActiveActive share
Mistral Large 3 (December 2025)675B41B6.1%
Mistral Large 4 (October 2026)1.05T52B5.0%

The model grew 56%; the active part grew only 27%. Mistral's launch post and model card don't say how many experts Large 4 has, how many are picked per token, or what its "granular Mixture-of-Experts architecture" means in detail, so I don't guess.

By Mistral's own numbers, the preview scores 61.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, solves 93% of Cybench's 40 security challenges, and got 3.74 out of 5 from human raters judging coding quality (second of five models).

The catch: sleeping experts still take memory

Unpicked experts cost no compute, but they must still be stored and loaded: the Mixtral paper notes that "the memory costs for serving Mixtral are proportional to its sparse parameter count", the total. All 1.05 trillion numbers of Large 4 must be in memory; at 2 bytes each (16-bit), that's about 2,100 GB. (Mistral hasn't published a file size; that's my arithmetic. Storing numbers with fewer bits is what quantization does.)

The whole idea in one line

1 trillion parameters does not mean 1 trillion per token. Large 4 stores about 1 trillion and each token touches about 1/20 of it. Mistral said at launch that the weights would follow "by the end of the month".
